How the solver works

The method by its exact name, and what it cannot do, in the same place.

What this is

Full-composition Gibbs energy minimisation. Every gas species and every allowed condensed phase is an unknown. The total Gibbs energy

μi = μ°i(T) + RT ln[yiP/P°] (gases)
μi = μ°i(T) (condensed, unit activity)
Gtotal = Σ ni μi

is minimised subject to elemental balance and non-negative moles:

minimise Gtotal
subject to A·n = A·nfeed
and ni ≥ 0

The phase assemblage is an output. Nothing tells the solver which oxide should be stable; it finds out by putting them all in and letting the minimum decide. That is the whole reason for doing it this way instead of comparing a gas against a table of phase boundaries.

What this is not

It is not a bisection on an oxygen chemical potential, and it is not a walk down a ladder of phase boundaries. That route exists, it is roughly two hundred times faster, and it is in this page — on the Verification tab, where it belongs. It is not what any number on the Results tab came from.

The distinction matters because the two agree almost everywhere. That is exactly what makes a value from the wrong one impossible to catch by reading it, so the two are kept apart in the code and every result carries method: "gibbs_min".

Why the degrees of freedom are small

With a CO2/H2 feed the gas holds C, H and O, and the solid adds one metal. Fixing which condensed phases are present leaves three or four independent composition variables: the shift between CO2/H2 and CO/H2O, methane formation, molecular O2, and the oxygen handed to or taken from the solid. That is a small enough problem to solve deterministically rather than hopefully.

Solver state

The panel on the left reports how many starting points were tried, how many iterations ran, the wall time, and the elemental-balance residual on the answer. The Element balance tab shows that residual per element. A residual there means the optimiser left the feasible set, which is a different failure from finding the wrong minimum inside it — both are possible, and only one of them is what that check catches.

Known defect, being fixed. The present build represents an absent phase by a very small number rather than by zero, because the extents are carried in logarithmic coordinates and the logarithm of zero does not exist. Absent phases therefore come back at around 10−26 to 10−17 percent instead of at nothing, and telling that residue apart from a phase that is genuinely present in a trace amount currently needs a second argument rather than being read straight off the answer. The replacement enumerates which phases are present, holds the absent ones at exactly zero, and compares the total Gibbs energy across those cases. Until that lands, treat any reduction below about 10−10 percent on this page as zero.

Derivation

1. Chemical potentials

Gases follow the NIST-JANAF Shomate form, with t = T/1000:

Cp° = A + Bt + Ct² + Dt³ + E/t²
H°(T) = At + Bt²/2 + Ct³/3 + Dt⁴/4 − E/t + F′
S°(T) = A ln t + Bt + Ct²/2 + Dt³/3 − E/(2t²) + G
μ°(T) = H°(T) − T·S°(T)

F′ = F − H + ΔfH°298 is folded in when the coefficients are exported, so this returns the potential on the elemental reference rather than relative to 298 K. The evaluator itself is the one from the WGS tools page, unchanged.

CALPHAD condensed phases use a different heat-capacity form and are integrated from 298.15 K, entering a transition enthalpy at each range boundary:

Magnéli: Cp = k₀ + k₁T−1/2 + k₂T−2 + k₃T−3
poly: Cp = a + bT + cT² + dT−2
H(T) = ΔfH°298 + Σ [ ΔHtrans + ∫CpdT ]
S(T) = S°298 + Σ [ ΔHtrans/Ttrans + ∫(Cp/T)dT ]

Both H and S step at a transition while G stays continuous, which is why an error there hides: the equilibrium uses G. Dropping ΔHtrans moves μ for Ti3O5 by about 35 kJ/mol at 1500 °C without breaking anything visible.

2. Reaction-extent coordinates

One component is chosen per element — here the charge as loaded, CO2, H2, H2O and the host oxide — and every remaining species is formed from them by one reaction, carrying one extent ξ:

n = n0(b) + Δn(ξ), Δnfree = ξ, Δncomp = −(AK−1AD)ξ
CO = CO2 + H2 − H2O
CH4 = CO2 + 4H2 − 2H2O
Ti10O19 = 10 TiO2 + H2 − H2O
Ce2O3 = 2 CeO2 + H2 − H2O

The change of variables is exact: same objective, same feasible set. It removes the elemental constraints by construction — they are satisfied identically, not enforced — and it lets the constant part of G be dropped analytically:

Grel(ξ) = G(n(ξ)) − G(n(0))
= ξ·ΔG°form + RT·[ mixing(n) − mixing(n0) ]
ΔG°form = μ°free + (AK−1AD)Tμ°comp

That is not a refinement. Under a sealed inert gas the oxide leaves stoichiometry by a mole fraction near 10−38, and in species coordinates n(TiO2) = nTi − 10ξ rounds to nTi exactly — a double carries sixteen digits and the gap is thirty-nine. The extent survives only if it is never subtracted from something large.

3. The mixing term

Written so a small change against a large reference never becomes a difference of two logarithms:

Δmix = Σgas [ ni,0·log1p(Δni/ni,0) + Δni·ln ni ]
− ng,0·log1p(δ/ng,0) − δ·ln ng + δ·ln P

4. The gradient

Analytic, because finite differences are useless on an objective that can sit at 10−19 kJ. The terms in ∂ng/∂ξ cancel identically, since the mole fractions sum to one:

∂G/∂ξj = ΔG°form,j + RT Σgas νij ln(yiP)

5. Oxygen potential — verification only

The metal does not enter the gas below about 2000 K, so the solid exchanges nothing with it but oxygen. Each phase boundary is then a plain function of temperature, with no optimiser in it:

μO = μ°(H2O) − μ°(H2) + RT ln(yH2O/yH2)
μOcrit(higher→lower) = [ n·μ°(higher) − μ°(lower) ] / ΔO
μO = ½[ μ°(O2) + RT ln pO2 ]

Two phases in contact fix μO exactly. So where the minimisation leaves two phases together, its gas has to land on a number computed from the phase data alone — a test of the minimiser using nothing the minimiser produced. Reporting this route instead would turn that test into a number compared against itself.
